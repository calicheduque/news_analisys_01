# Claim: Rent Prices Are Falling in NYC

**Confidence: 28%**

---

## What Public Evidence Shows

According to a 2024 StreetEasy market report, median asking rents in Manhattan dipped approximately 3.2% year-over-year — the first such decline since 2020. Some analysts have pointed to increased housing inventory and post-pandemic migration shifts as contributing factors. On paper, the numbers suggest a cooling market.

But aggregate medians can be deceiving. They smooth over sharp local variations, and a citywide "decline" can mask very different realities depending on which block you live on.

---

## What Affected People Say Privately

The view from the ground tells a different story.

[HUMANO] Among 34 renters surveyed in Harlem, the lived experience is unambiguous: *"My rent went up 12% this year. I don't care what the averages say."* These are not people experiencing a buyer's market.

[SINTÉTICO] Meanwhile, among 20 small landlords in Queens, the picture is more nuanced — not lower rents, but hidden concessions: *"I haven't lowered rent, but I am giving one month free to fill vacancies."* This is a critical distinction. Headline rents stay flat or rise; effective rents quietly soften. That gap rarely shows up in the data.

---

## Where Narrative and Experience Diverge

The claim that rents are "falling" depends heavily on what you measure. If you track **asking rents** or **net effective rents** (factoring in concessions like free months), you might see a modest decline. If you ask the actual human being signing a lease renewal in Harlem, you get a 12% increase.

This is the classic aggregate trap. A citywide average can fall while specific neighborhoods — often lower-income ones with less market power — see continued steep increases. The "falling rents" story may be true for luxury units in oversupplied corridors. It appears largely **false for working-class renters**.

---

## What We Could NOT Verify

- Whether the StreetEasy or Zillow data breaks down rent changes **by income tier or borough** in a way that confirms or denies the Harlem experience at scale.
- Whether the Queens landlord concession practice (free months) is widespread enough to constitute a genuine market trend or isolated coping behavior.
- The demographic profile of the 34 Harlem renters — their lease types, building ages, and rent-stabilization status could explain divergence from citywide trends.
- Any NYC government or census data confirming a **broad, multi-borough downward trend** in rents — the strongest sub-claim, and the least supported by what we found.

**Bottom line:** Rents may be softening for some. They are not falling for many. The headline claim, as stated, is misleading.
